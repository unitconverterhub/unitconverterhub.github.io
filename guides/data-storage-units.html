Bytes, Kilobytes, Gigabytes: Data Storage Units Explained

UnitConverter guides · Measurement

A photo is three megabytes, a film is two gigabytes, a hard drive is one terabyte — data units are part of everyday language, but few people know what they actually mean. A byte is eight bits, a kilobyte is a thousand bytes (or is it 1,024?), and the difference between the two definitions is the reason a one-terabyte drive never holds a full terabyte. This guide explains the units, the decimal-versus-binary trap, and how to work out how much storage you really need.

Bits and bytes

The bit is the smallest unit of digital information: a single binary digit, zero or one. Eight bits make a byte, and a byte can represent one character of text, a number from 0 to 255, or a single pixel's brightness. File sizes are measured in bytes, while internet speeds are measured in bits per second — which is why a 100-megabit connection downloads a 100-megabyte file in about eight seconds, not one. The factor of eight between bits and bytes is the most common source of confusion in all of computing.

A kilobyte is a thousand bytes in the decimal system used by storage manufacturers, and 1,024 bytes in the binary system used by operating systems. The difference grows with each step up the ladder: a megabyte is a million bytes or 1,048,576, a gigabyte is a billion or 1,073,741,824, and a terabyte is a trillion or 1,099,511,627,776. The binary units have official names — kibibyte, mebibyte, gibibyte, tebibyte — but almost nobody uses them, which keeps the confusion alive.

How big is a file, really?

A plain text email is a few kilobytes. A high-quality photo from a modern phone is three to eight megabytes. A three-minute song is three to five megabytes as an MP3 and thirty megabytes as a lossless file. A standard-definition film is about 700 megabytes, a high-definition film is two to four gigabytes, and a 4K film can be twenty gigabytes or more. A typical smartphone app is fifty to two hundred megabytes, and a modern video game can be fifty to one hundred and fifty gigabytes.

Video is the hungriest of all: an hour of 1080p video at a normal bitrate is about two gigabytes, an hour of 4K video is about seven, and an hour of raw professional footage can be hundreds of gigabytes. If you record a lot of video, storage disappears fast — a phone with 128 gigabytes holds about eighteen hours of 4K video and not much else. The practical rule is to buy twice the storage you think you need, because files only get bigger.

The terabyte trap

The most annoying data conversion is the one between the drive you bought and the drive your computer shows. A hard drive labelled one terabyte contains one trillion bytes, but your operating system divides by 1,024 at each step, so it reports about 931 gigabytes. Nothing is wrong and nothing is missing — the two systems simply use different definitions of gigabyte. The gap grows with size: a four-terabyte drive shows as 3.64 terabytes, and an eight-terabyte drive as 7.28.

Manufacturers are not cheating: they use the decimal definition, which is the official SI meaning of the prefixes, and they print the byte count in small print on the box. Operating systems use the binary definition because computers work in powers of two. Some newer systems now show both, and the storage industry has slowly started labelling drives in both units. Until the confusion is resolved, the safest approach is to compare drives by their byte count, not by their headline size.

How much storage do you need?

For a phone, 128 gigabytes is enough for most people who stream music and video and back up photos to the cloud. Heavy photographers and gamers should choose 256 or 512 gigabytes. For a laptop, 256 gigabytes is the minimum for comfortable use, 512 is the sweet spot, and a terabyte is worth it if you edit video or keep a large media library. For backups, buy a drive at least twice the size of the data you are backing up, so you have room for multiple versions.

Internet speed is measured in megabits per second, so divide by eight to get megabytes per second. A 100-megabit connection downloads at about 12.5 megabytes per second, which means a two-gigabyte film takes about three minutes. A 25-megabit connection is enough for 4K streaming, which needs about 25 megabits per second, but only just — any other device using the connection at the same time will cause buffering. When choosing a broadband plan, think in megabits and divide by eight to picture the real download speed.

Frequently asked questions

How many bytes in a kilobyte?
1,000 by the decimal definition; 1,024 by the binary definition used by operating systems.

Why does a 1 TB drive show 931 GB?
The drive maker counts a gigabyte as a billion bytes; your computer counts it as 1,073,741,824 bytes.

How many bits in a byte?
Eight bits make one byte.
