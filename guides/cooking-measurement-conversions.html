Cooking Measures: Cups, Grams, Millilitres and Tablespoons Explained

UnitConverter guides · Measurement

Few things frustrate a cook more than finding a perfect recipe written in the wrong measurement system. An American recipe calls for cups and tablespoons, a European one for grams and millilitres, and an old family recipe for a pinch, a knob and a teacup. Converting between them is not just arithmetic: a cup of flour and a cup of honey weigh very different amounts, and a tablespoon means something different in Sydney than it does in New York. This guide explains how cooking measures actually work and how to convert them reliably.

Volume measures: cups, spoons and fluid ounces

The cup is the most common volume measure in American cooking, and it holds 236.6 millilitres — usually rounded to 240. A metric cup, used in Australia and in many international recipes, holds exactly 250 millilitres, so the two cups differ by about 5 percent. A US tablespoon holds 14.8 millilitres, a teaspoon holds 4.9 millilitres, and there are three teaspoons in a tablespoon and sixteen tablespoons in a cup. A US fluid ounce is 29.6 millilitres, and there are eight fluid ounces in a cup.

Weight measures: grams, ounces and pounds

Weight is the most reliable way to measure ingredients, because it does not depend on how tightly you pack a cup or how finely you chop an onion. One ounce is 28.35 grams, one pound is 453.6 grams, and one kilogram is 2.2 pounds. Most modern recipes from Europe, Asia and professional kitchens give weights in grams, and the best baking books now give weights in both grams and ounces. A kitchen scale that measures in grams costs very little and will improve your cooking more than any other single purchase.

Converting a volume-based recipe to weights is worth the effort for baking. A cup of plain flour weighs about 120 grams if spooned lightly into the cup and levelled, but up to 150 grams if scooped directly from the bag. A cup of granulated sugar is about 200 grams, a cup of brown sugar packed firmly is about 220 grams, and a cup of butter is 227 grams — exactly half a pound, which is why American butter is sold in convenient half-cup sticks. Keep a printed conversion chart inside a kitchen cupboard door and you will never need to guess again.

Why weighing beats measuring

Professional kitchens weigh everything because it is faster, cleaner and more consistent. Weighing flour directly into the mixing bowl takes seconds and leaves no measuring cup to wash, and the result is the same every time regardless of who is cooking. Measuring by volume introduces variation at every step: how hard you press the flour down, whether you level the top, how humid the day is. For bread, where the ratio of water to flour determines the crumb, that variation is the difference between a beautiful loaf and a brick.

The same logic applies to liquids, though the stakes are lower because a millilitre of water weighs exactly one gram. Milk, oil and cream are close enough to water that weighing them in grams works just as well as measuring them in millilitres. The only ingredients that still deserve spoons are the tiny ones: baking powder, yeast, spices and salt, where a gram or two makes a real difference and a scale may not be sensitive enough. For those, keep a set of measuring spoons and level each spoonful with the back of a knife.

Converting recipes between systems

When converting a whole recipe, convert each ingredient individually rather than scaling the whole thing by a single factor, because different ingredients have different densities. Start with the ingredients that matter most — flour, sugar, butter and liquids — and convert those by weight. For the rest, approximate freely: an onion is an onion whether the recipe calls it medium or 150 grams. Write the converted amounts in the margin of the recipe so you only have to do the work once.

Oven temperatures need conversion too, and they are the easiest part to get wrong. A recipe that says 350 Fahrenheit means 177 Celsius, which most ovens round to 180. Gas mark 4 is the same temperature. If your oven runs hot or cold — and most do by ten or fifteen degrees — an oven thermometer is a better investment than any conversion chart. Finally, remember that baking times do not change when you convert units; a cake that bakes for 35 minutes at 180 Celsius still bakes for 35 minutes, whatever units the recipe was written in.

Frequently asked questions

How many grams in a cup of flour?
About 120 grams when spooned and levelled; up to 150 grams if scooped.

Is a US cup the same as a metric cup?
No — a US cup is 237 ml and a metric cup is 250 ml.

How many millilitres in a tablespoon?
15 ml in the US and UK; 20 ml in Australia.
