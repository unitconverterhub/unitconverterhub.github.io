Clothing Sizes Compared: US, UK and EU Sizes Explained

UnitConverter guides · Measurement

Clothing sizes are even more confusing than shoe sizes, because they are not measurements at all but arbitrary numbers assigned by each country's fashion industry. A US women's size 8 is a UK size 12 and a European size 38. A men's 34-inch waist is a European size 44. And the same nominal size can differ by several centimetres between brands. This guide explains the main systems, how to measure yourself, and how to shop across borders without disappointment.

Women's clothing sizes

Women's sizing differs most between countries. A US size 2 is a UK size 6 and a European size 34. A US size 8 is a UK size 12 and a European size 38. A US size 14 is a UK size 18 and a European size 44. The pattern is simple: UK sizes are four numbers higher than US sizes, and European sizes are roughly thirty higher than US sizes for dresses and tops. But these are only rough equivalents, because each country cuts its clothes to a different body shape — American clothes tend to be cut roomier, European clothes slimmer.

Men's clothing sizes

Men's sizing is more logical, because it is usually based on actual body measurements. A shirt size is the neck circumference in inches or centimetres: a 15.5-inch neck is a 39-centimetre neck. Trouser sizes give the waist and the inside leg: a 34 by 32 means a 34-inch waist and a 32-inch inside leg. Suit jackets are sized by the chest in inches, so a 40 jacket fits a 40-inch chest. European men's sizes use centimetres: a 52 jacket is a 40-inch chest, because 52 centimetres is roughly half the chest circumference in centimetres.

The conversion is straightforward once you know the pattern. To convert a men's chest from inches to a European size, multiply the inches by 2.54 and then divide by two, rounding to the nearest even number. A 40-inch chest is 101.6 centimetres, half of which is 50.8, so the European size is 50 or 52. For trousers, a 34-inch waist is 86 centimetres, and the European size is usually given as the waist in centimetres divided by two, so 43. The safest approach is always to check the brand's chart, because cuts vary.

How to measure yourself

To find your size in any system, take three measurements with a soft tape measure. For the chest or bust, measure around the fullest part with the tape level and your arms relaxed. For the waist, measure at your natural waistline — usually just above the navel — without pulling the tape tight. For the hips, measure around the fullest part. For trousers, measure the inside leg from the crotch to the ankle bone. Take each measurement twice, standing relaxed, and write the numbers down in both inches and centimetres.

Measure over the underwear you would normally wear with the garment, and do not hold your breath or suck in your stomach — the clothes need to fit the real you, not the aspirational you. If you are between sizes, consider the fabric: stretchy fabrics forgive a smaller size, while structured fabrics such as wool suiting need the larger size. For online shopping, compare your measurements with the brand's size chart and read reviews, which often say whether a garment runs small or large.

Why sizes vary and how to cope

Sizes vary because there is no international standard for clothing, and because brands deliberately cut their clothes to flatter their target customer. A brand aimed at young, slim customers will cut its size 10 smaller than a brand aimed at older customers, and both will label it a 10. Fast fashion brands are notorious for inconsistency, with the same size varying between different garments from the same shop. This is frustrating, but it is the reality of an industry that has never agreed on a standard.

The practical strategy is to stop trusting size numbers and start trusting measurements. Know your three key measurements in centimetres, check the brand's chart every time you order, and buy from retailers with easy returns when trying a new brand. When shopping in a foreign country, try clothes on rather than converting in your head — a fitting room is worth more than any conversion table. And remember that a good tailor can fix almost any fit problem for a small price, which is often cheaper than returning an otherwise perfect garment.
